# Pregnancy & Postpartum Training

> **TL;DR** — In an **uncomplicated** pregnancy, exercise — including **resistance training** — is safe and actively good for both mother and baby, and every major obstetric body now recommends it rather than warning against it. But this page is **educational, not medical advice**, and it does **not** apply to high-risk or complicated pregnancies: your OB or midwife clears you to train and sets the limits — always. The training itself barely changes in principle; what changes is the *goal*. Shift from **building** to **maintaining**: keep lifting, but train comfortably shy of failure, drop load and volume as you grow, skip anything with a fall, impact, or belly-compression risk, avoid max breath-holding, and don't overheat. **Postpartum**, come back gradually and only once cleared — rebuild from the pelvic floor and deep core up, and progress by how you feel, not by the calendar. Learn the warning signs, and stop if they appear.

**Quick recommendations**
- **Get medical clearance first, and let it govern everything here.** This page never overrides your OB/midwife. Some conditions make exercise unsafe; only a clinician who knows your pregnancy can rule them out. **[Grade D]**
- **Keep training — exercise is recommended, not merely tolerated.** For an uncomplicated pregnancy, aim for about **150 minutes/week** of moderate activity, and **resistance training is explicitly included** as safe and beneficial. **[Grade B]**
- **Change the goal from *build* to *maintain*.** Preserving your strength and muscle through pregnancy is a win. Train with more reps in reserve than usual (leave several — never grind to failure), and stop sets while they still feel controlled. **[Grade D]**
- **Modify as you grow.** After roughly the first trimester avoid prolonged **flat-on-your-back** (supine) work; avoid heavy **breath-holding/straining** (the Valsalva maneuver); skip exercises with **fall, impact, or contact** risk or that compress the belly; and manage **heat and hydration**. **[Grade D]**
- **The payoff is real and medical.** Prenatal exercise lowers the risk of **gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension and pre-eclampsia**, excessive weight gain, and **prenatal and postpartum depression**. **[Grade B]**
- **Return postpartum gradually, and only when cleared.** Rebuild the **pelvic floor and deep core first**, then reload; progress by symptoms, not a fixed timeline (and expect longer after a caesarean or a complicated delivery). **[Grade D]**
- **Stop and seek care for warning signs** — vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking, regular painful contractions, chest pain, calf pain or swelling, dizziness, or headache. **[Grade D]**

## Practical Application

**First, the boundary.** This is general education for people with an **uncomplicated, low-risk** pregnancy who already train or want to. It is not medical advice, and it deliberately stops where medicine begins. Pregnancy has genuine **contraindications** to exercise — some absolute (certain heart or lung conditions, placenta previa after mid-pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, cervical insufficiency, and others) and some relative — and only your obstetric provider can tell you which, if any, apply to you. **Get cleared before you train, and treat their guidance as the constraint that outranks everything below.**

**The principle barely changes; the goal does.** Pregnancy does not rewrite how muscle works, but it changes what you should be *chasing*. This is a **maintain-and-defend** phase, not a personal-best phase. If you hold onto most of your strength and muscle and stay active and healthy through the pregnancy, that is a complete success — and it sets up a much easier postpartum return. So keep the lifts you know, but **back off the intensity of effort**: leave several reps in reserve, end sets while the bar speed and your form are still clean, and never train to failure or grind a rep. The old cue "could you do 3–5 more?" is a good floor here.

**Trimester by trimester, in broad strokes.**
- **First trimester:** often you can train much as before, adjusting for fatigue and nausea. Start building the habit of stopping short of failure now.
- **Second trimester:** as the bump grows, drop **prolonged supine** exercises (lying flat on your back can compress a major vein and make some people light-headed — incline the bench or swap the movement). Expect to trade some barbell work for machines, dumbbells, and cables that fit your changing body and balance.
- **Third trimester:** load and volume usually come down as comfort, balance, and breathing dictate. Keep moving; reduce range or load rather than stopping, and favor stable, low-skill variations.

**A few specific modifications worth naming.**
- **Breathing over bracing.** Heavy **Valsalva** (holding your breath and straining against a max load) spikes blood pressure and intra-abdominal pressure — keep loads submaximal and breathe through reps. (See [Bracing & breathing](../09-getting-started/bracing-and-breathing.md), which already flags pregnancy for this reason.)
- **Protect the belly and your balance.** Avoid movements with a real **fall or impact** risk, contact, or that press directly on the abdomen. As your center of gravity shifts, stable machines and supported positions beat free-standing, balance-heavy lifts.
- **Core work changes.** Skip loaded spinal flexion / hard crunching later on; favor gentle, breath-coordinated deep-core and pelvic-floor work. If you notice a **doming or coning** down the midline of your belly under load, that movement is too much for now.
- **Heat and hydration.** Don't train to overheating; keep sessions cool and well-hydrated.

**Postpartum: rebuild from the inside out.** Recovery from birth is its own process, and there is no universal green-light date — many people are cleared for gradual return around **six weeks**, but it is **longer after a caesarean or a complicated delivery**, and the clearance is your provider's to give. When you do return, start below where you left off and **rebuild the pelvic floor and deep core first** — these took the load of pregnancy and delivery and are the foundation for everything heavier. **Diastasis recti** (an abdominal-wall separation) and pelvic-floor symptoms are common and usually improve with graded work; leaking, heaviness/pressure, or pain are signals to slow down and, if they persist, to see a **pelvic-health physiotherapist**. Progress by how your body responds week to week, not by rushing back to old numbers. If you are breastfeeding, fuel and hydrate accordingly. Muscle you built before doesn't vanish — [muscle memory](returning-lifters-and-special-populations.md) means it comes back faster than it first went on, so there is no need to train like a raw beginner or to hurry.

## The Evidence

**Exercise, including resistance training, is recommended in uncomplicated pregnancy.** The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises that women with uncomplicated pregnancies be **encouraged to engage in aerobic and strength-conditioning exercise** before, during, and after pregnancy, and that physical activity is associated with minimal risks and clear benefits in this population[^acog-2020-pregnancy-exercise]. **[Grade B]** The 2019 Canadian guideline for physical activity throughout pregnancy makes the dose concrete: accumulate at least **150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity across at least three days per week**, and it specifically recommends **combining aerobic with resistance training**, which it found more beneficial than aerobic activity alone[^mottola-2018-pregnancy-guideline]. **[Grade B]** These are recommendations *for* training, framed by a list of contraindications for the minority for whom it isn't safe — which is why medical clearance comes first.

**The benefits are substantive and medical, not cosmetic.** A systematic review and meta-analysis found that prenatal exercise was associated with **reduced odds of gestational diabetes mellitus, gestational hypertension, and pre-eclampsia**[^davenport-2018-prenatal-gdm-htn]. **[Grade B]** A companion meta-analysis found that prenatal exercise **reduced the severity of prenatal depressive symptoms and the odds of prenatal depression**, with signals extending into the postpartum period[^davenport-2018-prenatal-depression]. **[Grade B]** In other words, staying active through pregnancy meaningfully lowers the risk of some of its most common complications — a strong reason to keep training when you safely can.

## Key Uncertainties & Nuance
- **The evidence base is about health outcomes, not hypertrophy.** These trials measure gestational diabetes, blood pressure, weight gain, mood, and delivery outcomes — **not** how much muscle you build or keep. So the honest framing for a lifter is "training through pregnancy is safe and healthy, and maintaining is realistic," *not* "here is how to grow during pregnancy," which simply isn't well studied.
- **"How close to failure is safe" is not precisely quantified.** The guidance to train well shy of failure and avoid maximal straining is a sensible, conservative extrapolation (submaximal effort, controlled breathing), not a number derived from hypertrophy trials in pregnant lifters.
- **High-risk pregnancies are excluded from this evidence and this page.** Everything here assumes an uncomplicated pregnancy cleared by a clinician; complications change the calculus entirely and are a medical matter.
- **Individual variation is enormous.** Energy, nausea, pelvic pain, balance, and recovery differ hugely between people and across a single pregnancy. Adjust load, range, and volume to how you actually feel that day rather than to a plan.
- **Postpartum timelines are individual.** Return-to-exercise readiness depends on delivery type, complications, pelvic-floor recovery, and symptoms — the "six weeks" figure is a common checkpoint, not a universal green light.

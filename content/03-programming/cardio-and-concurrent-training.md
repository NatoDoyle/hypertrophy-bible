# Cardio & Concurrent Training

> **TL;DR** — Cardio and muscle growth are compatible, and the feared "**interference effect**" is much smaller than its reputation. At the whole-muscle level, adding cardio to lifting costs you **essentially nothing**; at the muscle-fibre level there is a **small** cost, and almost all of it belongs to **running**. Cardio also *helps* you grow in ways this page used to ignore: it buys you the energy headroom to eat more, defends the expenditure that collapses during a diet, and keeps you healthy enough to still be training in ten years. A sane default is **7,000–8,000 steps a day plus 2–3 sessions of 20–40 minutes** on something low-impact. The cost is a **gradient, not a cliff** — so the useful skill is reading your own signals, which the self-check below gives you.

**Quick recommendations**
- **Do the cardio.** All-cause mortality keeps falling with more daily steps up to about **8,000–10,000 a day under age 60**, and **6,000–8,000 over 60**. Most of that return comes from walking, which has no measured cost to muscle. **[Grade B]**
- **Add 2–3 easy-to-moderate sessions of 20–40 minutes** on a bike, rower, elliptical or incline treadmill if you want cardiorespiratory fitness on top of steps. That dose sits low on the interference gradient. **[Grade D]**
- **Prefer low-impact work to running** when legs are the priority — running is the one modality with a measured fibre-level cost. **[Grade B]**
- **Don't let cardio create your calorie deficit.** Food first, steps second, structured cardio third — see [energy balance](../04-nutrition/energy-balance.md). **[Grade D]**
- **Keep hard leg cardio off leg day and the day before it.** Same muscles, same recovery budget. **[Grade D]**
- **Re-run the self-check every month or so.** Nobody can tell you your personal ceiling; your own lifts can. **[Grade D]**

## Practical Application

**Where cardio helps you grow.** The case for cardio is not just "heart health, sadly at the expense of gains" — several of its effects point the right way for hypertrophy.

- **It buys you food.** Muscle is built on a calorie surplus, and the practical limit on a surplus is how much fat you are willing to add. Raising your expenditure means the same body composition at a higher intake, which makes hitting protein and carbohydrate targets easier rather than harder (see [carbohydrates, fat & hydration](../04-nutrition/carbohydrates-fat-and-hydration.md)). **[Grade D]**
- **It defends your expenditure in a diet.** During weight loss, total daily expenditure falls by more than the lost tissue explains, and a large part of that is reduced spontaneous movement. Deliberate activity partly offsets it — which is why steps matter most exactly when you are [building muscle in a deficit](../04-nutrition/building-muscle-in-a-deficit.md). **[Grade C]**
- **At the untrained end, aerobic work is itself a growth stimulus.** In previously untrained and older people, aerobic exercise measurably increases muscle protein synthesis and muscle size on its own. This does not mean cycling will grow an experienced lifter's quads — it means the "cardio eats muscle" story is backwards for beginners and for anyone protecting muscle later in life (see [training status](../06-individualization/training-status.md)). **[Grade C]**
- **It keeps you in the game.** You build muscle across decades, not months. The single largest threat to a twenty-year training career is not a stalled squat, it is a cardiovascular event. **[Grade B]**
- **It plausibly raises work capacity.** Better aerobic fitness should mean faster recovery between sets and less fade across a long session. This is mechanistically sensible and widely reported by lifters, but it is not established by hypertrophy trials. **[Grade D]**

**Where cardio costs you.** Five levers decide whether the cost is trivial or real.

| Lever | Low cost | Higher cost | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Modality** | Walking, cycling, rowing, elliptical | Running; hard intervals | **[Grade B]** |
| **Weekly dose** | 2–3 shorter sessions | Many long sessions — the cost rises continuously with frequency and duration | **[Grade B]** |
| **Muscle overlap** | Work that spares what you are trying to grow | Work that hammers the same legs you squat with | **[Grade D]** |
| **Proximity** | Separate days, or roughly six hours apart | Hard leg cardio immediately before training legs | **[Grade D]** |
| **What is at stake** | Muscle *size*, the most protected outcome | Explosive power and maximal strength, the first to suffer | **[Grade B]** |

Cardio also spends from the same recovery budget as lifting, alongside sleep debt, life stress and a calorie deficit. Any one of these is usually survivable; stacked together they are what actually stalls people (see [stress, recovery & overtraining](../05-recovery/stress-recovery-and-overtraining.md) and [sleep](../05-recovery/sleep.md)).

**The dose that does the job.** These ranges are practical models, not measured constants. The step figures track the health evidence; the session figures are coaching consensus placed deliberately low on the interference gradient.

| Goal | Steps/day | Sessions/week | Minutes each |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gaining muscle** | 7,000–8,000 | 2–3 | 20–40 |
| **Strength** | 7,000–8,000 | 2–3 | 20–40 |
| **Losing fat** | 8,000–12,000 | 3–5 | 20–45 |
| **Recomposition** | 8,000–10,000 | 2–4 | 20–40 |

Two rules matter more than the exact numbers. **In a gaining phase, do not drop to zero** — a maintenance floor of cardio costs you nothing measurable and keeps fitness (and appetite) where you want them. **In a fat-loss phase, add steps before you add sessions**, because steps buy expenditure at almost no recovery cost, and cardio you have to recover from competes directly with the lifting that protects your muscle.

**The words people use for these intensities.** The 20–40 minute easy-to-moderate sessions above are what's usually sold as **"zone 2"** or **"LISS"** (low-intensity steady state): a conversational pace you could hold for the whole session and still speak in full sentences. **"HIIT"** means the opposite — short, hard intervals with recovery between them; on the evidence below it's hypertrophy-neutral but carries the small lower-body strength cost, so it's a reasonable pick when you're short on time and a worse one during a leg-priority block. **"VO2max"** is a lab measure of aerobic capacity, and **"NEAT"** is all the movement that isn't a workout — the walking, fidgeting and standing that your step target is really about (see [energy balance](../04-nutrition/energy-balance.md)). **[Grade D]** — these are definitions, not prescriptions.

The labels don't change the advice. This page deliberately doses cardio by **time, steps and modality** rather than by heart-rate zone, because the levers that actually showed up in the evidence are *which modality*, *how much*, and *how close to leg day* — not which band your heart rate sat in. If you like training to a zone, the talk test above is close enough for this purpose.

**Is cardio helping or hurting you? The self-check.** The honest answer is that nobody can hand you your personal ceiling, because it depends on your recovery capacity, your job, your sleep and your age. What you can do is read the signals.

| What to watch | Cardio is paying off | Cardio is costing you |
|---|---|---|
| Lower- vs upper-body lift progression | Both keep climbing | Legs go flat or slide while upper body keeps climbing |
| Weekly sets vs your volume landmarks | Stalls happen at or above MRV | A muscle stalls while its sets sit inside the productive range |
| Bodyweight vs your goal | Tracks the direction you want | Drifting down while you are trying to gain |
| Daily readiness check-ins | Steady or rising across a block | Block average drifts low and stays there |
| Reps held across sets | You hold reps, or need less rest for them | Reps fall off sharply from first set to last |
| Session RPE at unchanged loads | The same weights feel the same or easier | The same weights feel harder week over week |
| Resting heart rate over weeks | Trending down | Trending up alongside the other signals |
| Appetite, sleep, wanting to train | All fine | Appetite suppressed, sleep broken, training a chore |

**Before you blame cardio, check your volume.** This is the single most useful discriminator, and it is easy to get backwards. If a muscle has stalled while its weekly hard sets sit **inside** its productive range, the limiter is probably *outside* the gym — recovery, food, or cardio load. If it has stalled at or above its maximum recoverable volume, that is a **lifting**-volume problem and cutting cardio will not fix it; see [volume](../01-training-variables/volume.md) and [volume progression & deloads](volume-progression-and-deloads.md). Work the rest of the [plateau playbook](../07-tracking/breaking-advanced-plateaus.md) in parallel, and track enough that these signals are visible at all ([what to track](../07-tracking/what-to-track.md)).

**Then run the test.** Halve your structured cardio for two to three weeks and hold everything else constant: same lifting volume, same calories, same sleep. If the stalled lifts start moving, cardio load was the limiter. Change one thing at a time or the test tells you nothing.

## The Evidence

**At the whole-muscle level, the interference effect has largely evaporated.** Schumann and colleagues' 2022 meta-analysis concluded that concurrent aerobic and strength training does **not** compromise muscle hypertrophy relative to strength training alone — essentially zero difference — although **explosive strength was slightly attenuated, particularly with same-session training**[^schumann-2022-concurrent-meta]. **[Grade B]** Murach and Bagley reached the same direction from the mechanistic literature, arguing the interference effect on human muscle growth is "not as compelling as previously thought" and that under some conditions concurrent training may even *augment* hypertrophy[^murach-2016-concurrent-contrary]. **[Grade C]**

**At the fibre level, a small cost survives — and it is mostly running.** Lundberg and colleagues' 2022 meta-analysis of 15 studies found a small overall reduction in fibre hypertrophy with concurrent training (standardised mean difference **−0.23**, 95% CI −0.46 to −0.00). Split by fibre type, neither reached significance on its own (type I **−0.34**, type II **−0.13**). The one clear effect was by modality: **type I fibre hypertrophy was impaired when the aerobic training was running (−0.81) but not when it was cycling**[^lundberg-2022-fiber-hypertrophy]. **[Grade B]** Critically, none of the other subgroup analyses — training frequency, training status, or same-session training **order** — showed any difference between groups. That is a direct check on some very confident coaching advice.

**The older, more alarming picture came from lower-quality studies, but its dose signal still stands.** Wilson and colleagues' 2012 meta-analysis found that adding aerobic training reduced hypertrophy and strength gains, that **running rather than cycling drove the decrements**, and that interference scaled with the amount of aerobic work: significant negative correlations for **frequency (−0.26 to −0.35)** and **duration (−0.29 to −0.75)**[^wilson-2012-concurrent-meta]. **[Grade B]** Note what this is and is not: a continuous relationship, not a threshold. The literature does not identify a number of minutes past which gains stop, which is why this page gives you a gradient and a self-check rather than a cliff edge.

**Intervals are their own case, and they complicate the modality story.** Sabag and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis found concurrent high-intensity interval training did **not** impair hypertrophy or upper-body strength, but did cost a little **lower-body strength** (ES **−0.248**). Awkwardly for the tidy version of the modality rule, it was **cycling**-based intervals that trended toward the larger decrement (−0.377), not running (−0.176)[^sabag-2018-hiit-concurrent-meta]. **[Grade B]**

**Mechanistically, the competition is real but conditional.** Endurance work activates AMPK signalling associated with energy stress, which can oppose the mTOR signalling that drives muscle protein synthesis; the practical size of that conflict depends on the training variables rather than being a fixed law[^fyfe-2014-interference-molecular]. **[Grade D]** In the other direction, aerobic training acutely and chronically alters muscle protein metabolism and induces hypertrophy in its own right, particularly in populations prone to muscle loss[^konopka-2014-aerobic-hypertrophy]. **[Grade C]**

**The health case is quantified.** Pooling 15 cohorts and 47,471 adults, Paluch and colleagues found all-cause mortality fell stepwise across step-count quartiles (hazard ratios **0.60**, **0.55** and **0.47** against the least active quartile), with the curve flattening around **6,000–8,000 steps a day in adults 60 and over** and **8,000–10,000 in adults under 60**[^paluch-2022-daily-steps-mortality]. **[Grade B]** And during weight loss, expenditure falls further than tissue loss predicts, with reduced non-exercise movement a major contributor — the reason steps are a defensive tool in a diet[^trexler-2014-metabolic-adaptation]. **[Grade C]**

## Key Uncertainties & Nuance
- **"Separate your sessions, and lift first" is consensus, not evidence.** It is cheap to follow and probably harmless, but the best fibre-level meta-analysis specifically tested same-session training order and found no difference. Treat it as a hedge; do not reorganise your life around it.
- **The modality rule is outcome-specific.** Running looks worse for fibre size; cycling intervals looked worse for lower-body strength. "Low-impact beats running" is a reasonable default for a lifter chasing size, not a universal law.
- **Size is the most protected outcome, power the least.** If you compete in anything explosive, be more conservative with same-session concurrent work than this page's defaults.
- **Almost all of this evidence runs 4–24 weeks, mostly in untrained or recreationally-active people.** Whether a small fibre-level decrement compounds over years of advanced training is genuinely unknown.
- **Individual recovery capacity is the real variable**, and no study can give you yours. That is precisely why the self-check and the halving test exist rather than a prescription you are supposed to trust.
- **"Cardio kills gains" is dead as a blanket claim.** The live questions are modality, dose and what else is draining you at the same time.

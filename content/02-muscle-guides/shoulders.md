# Shoulders (Deltoids)

> **TL;DR** — The deltoid is **three heads with three different programming needs**, and treating it as one muscle is why most people's shoulders lag. The **front delt** already gets hammered by every press you do and rarely needs direct work. The **side delt** — the one that builds width and the capped look — and the **rear delt** get almost nothing from compounds, so they're where dedicated volume pays. Roughly **8–20 sets a week for side delts** (they tolerate the most of any small muscle), **8–18 for rear delts**, and often **zero** direct front-delt work. Keep raises light and close to failure: on these muscles, effort beats load.

**Quick recommendations**
- **Side delts are the priority.** Start near **8 sets/week**, build toward **12–20**, ceiling around **24–26** ([side-delt landmarks](../../data/muscles/side-delts.json)) — the highest tolerance of any small muscle here. Spread over **2–4 sessions**. **[Grade C]**
- **Train rear delts directly.** ~**8–18 sets/week** across 2–3 sessions ([rear-delt landmarks](../../data/muscles/rear-delts.json)). Rows help but don't finish the job. **[Grade C]**
- **Add little or no direct front-delt work.** Its whole maintenance requirement is ~**4 sets/week** and pressing already clears that several times over ([front-delt landmarks](../../data/muscles/front-delts.json)). **[Grade C]**
- **Go light and near failure** on raises and flyes. Low loads taken close to failure build muscle comparably to heavy loads[^schoenfeld-2017-load-meta], and these are small muscles with poor leverage. **[Grade B]**
- **Don't agonise over dumbbell vs cable.** With range of motion matched, an 8-week trial trained one arm with each and found **no meaningful difference** in lateral-delt growth[^larsen-2025-lateral-raise-dumbbell-cable]. **[Grade B]** Pick what lets you train hard and consistently.
- **Count your pressing.** Overhead and incline work is front-delt volume — it goes inside the weekly total, not on top of it. **[Grade C]**

## Practical Application

The three heads share a name and almost nothing else. Picks below are ordered for **hypertrophy** — biasing the loaded stretch, then machine/cable stability where it lets you train closer to failure, then lower systemic fatigue — the same ranking the app's engine applies.

**Region 1 — Side delts (lateral head): the one that actually needs the work.** *Job: shoulder abduction — raising the arm out to the side. Almost nothing else you do trains it.*
1. [Cable lateral raise](../../data/exercises/cable-lateral-raise.json) — tension where a dumbbell has none: at the bottom, with the arm down. Easy to load in small jumps, which matters on a muscle that progresses in 1–2 kg steps.
2. [Lean-away lateral raise](../../data/exercises/lean-away-lateral-raise.json) — the dumbbell version of the same idea: hang off an upright so the bottom of the rep still has a load on it.
3. [Dumbbell lateral raise](../../data/exercises/dumbbell-lateral-raise.json) — the staple, and per the trial above, **not worse than a cable when the range matches**. Tension does fall to nearly nothing at the bottom, so don't rest there.
4. [Machine lateral raise](../../data/exercises/machine-lateral-raise.json) — nothing to stabilise and a pad to push against; excellent for taking a set genuinely to failure.
5. [Band lateral raise](../../data/exercises/band-lateral-raise.json) — the travel option; resistance is highest at the top, where the delt is shortest, so pair it with something loaded low.

*Cue:* **lead with the elbow**, not the hand, and stop around shoulder height — above that the traps take over. Lower slowly; the lowering half is where most of the tension you paid for actually lands.

**Region 2 — Rear delts: under-trained almost universally.** *Job: horizontal abduction and external rotation — moving the arm backward and rotating it out.*
1. [Cable rear-delt fly](../../data/exercises/cable-rear-delt-fly.json) — the standout: constant tension through a long arc, and the only common rear-delt movement loaded in the stretched position.
2. [Reverse pec-deck](../../data/exercises/reverse-pec-deck.json) — the easiest to do a lot of, and the easiest to push to failure safely.
3. [Face-pull](../../data/exercises/face-pull.json) — adds the external-rotation component; good for shoulder health as well as size.
4. [Chest-supported rear-delt row](../../data/exercises/chest-supported-rear-delt-row.json) — a rowing pattern with the elbows flared wide, which shifts the work off the lats and onto the rear delts.
5. [Dumbbell rear-delt fly](../../data/exercises/dumbbell-rear-delt-fly.json) / [prone reverse fly](../../data/exercises/prone-reverse-fly.json) — fine, but tension is near zero at the bottom.

*Cue:* think **elbows apart**, not hands back, and keep the weight light enough that the mid-back doesn't take over. Rear delts are one of the few places where "you're going too heavy" is nearly always the right diagnosis.

**Region 3 — Front delts: usually already done.** *Job: shoulder flexion and horizontal adduction — pressing and raising the arm forward.*

Every bench press, incline press, dip and push-up trains it hard. Its whole maintenance dose is around 4 sets a week, and a single pressing session clears that. If you want a dedicated press anyway — for strength, or because you like it — the ranked picks are [seated dumbbell shoulder press](../../data/exercises/seated-dumbbell-shoulder-press.json) (best stretch), [machine shoulder press](../../data/exercises/machine-shoulder-press.json) (lowest fatigue), [barbell overhead press](../../data/exercises/barbell-overhead-press.json) (most loadable, but the highest systemic cost on this page), or [Arnold press](../../data/exercises/arnold-press.json). Direct **front raises** are the most commonly wasted sets in the gym — [incline dumbbell front raise](../../data/exercises/incline-dumbbell-front-raise.json) at least loads the lengthened position if you insist.

**Putting a week together.** Lateral raises **2–4 times a week** — they recover fast, and spreading them beats one huge session; rear-delt work **2–3 times**, easiest to bolt onto pull days; front delts, whatever your pressing already gives you. A complete shoulder week can be as little as six lateral-raise sets and four rear-delt sets bolted onto an existing push/pull split, and that's still more delt work than most programmes contain.

**The universal levers still rule.** These picks simply apply the fundamentals every muscle obeys — enough [volume](../01-training-variables/volume.md), real [effort](../01-training-variables/proximity-to-failure.md) close to failure, and sound [exercise selection](../01-training-variables/exercise-selection-and-order.md), progressed over time — to this region. The rear delts also appear in the [Back guide](back.md), since every row trains them a little; the front delts take a share of everything in the [Chest guide](chest.md).

## The Evidence

Delt training is a clean application of general principles rather than a delt-specific literature — and it's worth being precise about which parts are actually tested.

**Volume drives growth** here as everywhere[^schoenfeld-2017-volume-dose-response] **[Grade A]**, which is the real argument for prioritising side and rear delts: not that they're special, but that compounds leave them near their *maintenance* dose while the same compounds push the front delt toward its ceiling. That asymmetry is a reasoned selection argument grounded in function, not a measured constant. **[Grade C]**

**Load can be light if effort is high.** Low- and high-load training produce comparable hypertrophy when sets are taken near failure[^schoenfeld-2017-load-meta] **[Grade B]** — which is why a heavy, swung lateral raise is a worse set than a light, controlled one.

**Implement choice matters less than it's sold as.** Twenty-four resistance-trained lifters did eight weeks of lateral raises, five sets to momentary failure twice a week, with **one arm on a cable and the other on dumbbells and the range of motion standardised between them**. Lateral-delt thickness rose 3.3–4.6%, and the analysis favoured *no difference* between implements — moderate support for the null on each site individually and extreme support in the multivariate model[^larsen-2025-lateral-raise-dumbbell-cable]. **[Grade B]**

Read that result precisely, because it is easy to over-extend: it tested **the implement with range of motion held constant**, and found the implement didn't matter. It did **not** test whether loading the lengthened position matters, because equalising range of motion removes exactly that variable. The broader range-of-motion evidence — that training at longer muscle lengths matches or beats short-range work — still stands[^kassiano-2023-rom-review]. **[Grade C]** The practical translation: the *reason* to like cables and lean-aways is that they make a full, loaded range easy to achieve, not that the cable itself is magic. A dumbbell raise through the same range is just as good.

## Key Uncertainties & Nuance
- **No trial has tested "should you do direct front-delt work?"** The recommendation to skip it is inference from the volume landmarks plus the fact that pressing loads the front delt heavily. It's sound reasoning, not a measured result, and it's why that line is Grade C. If your front delts genuinely lag, adding a few sets is not a mistake.
- **"3D delts" marketing oversells the complexity.** Cover abduction (side) and horizontal abduction/external rotation (rear) and the important work is done. There is no fourth head to isolate.
- **Lateral raise technique is easy to butcher.** Momentum and shrugging move the work to the traps. If the weight can only move with a heave, it's too heavy — see the load evidence above.
- **The side delt's high volume tolerance is a model-based estimate**, not a measured ceiling ([side-delt landmarks](../../data/muscles/side-delts.json) is Grade C). Build toward it from your own response rather than starting at 20 sets.
- **Overhead pressing is optional for hypertrophy.** It's valuable for strength and it's a genuinely good front-delt builder, but nothing breaks if your programme has incline pressing and lateral raises instead. It carries the highest systemic fatigue cost of anything on this page.

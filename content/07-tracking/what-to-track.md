# What to Track

> **TL;DR** — Track the things you can act on. The most useful metric is your **training performance** — the **load and reps** you do on your key exercises — because progressive overload is the engine of growth and this is the number you steer week to week. Track **bodyweight, tape measurements, and photos** as slower outcome checks, judged over **months**, not days, because they're noisy. Don't confuse the leading indicator (are my lifts progressing?) with the lagging one (am I bigger?).

**Quick recommendations**
- **Log load × reps** on your main exercises every session — this is the actionable metric. **[Grade A]**
- Track **weekly hard sets per muscle** — a *hard set* is a working set taken to about **0–4 reps in reserve**; warm-ups don't count[^baz-valle-2021-counting-sets]. Counting sets this way is the most practical valid way to measure your dose (see [Volume](../01-training-variables/volume.md)). **[Grade B]**
- Turn load × reps into **one comparable number** with an estimated 1RM, so 60 kg × 8 and 57.5 kg × 10 can actually be ranked. **[Grade C]**
- Check **bodyweight (trend), tape measurements, and photos** every few weeks — judge over long windows. **[Grade B]**
- Expect your **RIR calls to be least accurate far from failure** — a "4 RIR" is a much rougher guess than a "1 RIR"[^zourdos-2021-rir-accuracy]. **[Grade B]**
- Don't over-read **day-to-day** size/weight changes — they're mostly water, food, and glycogen. **[Grade B]**

## Practical Application

**Two tiers of metrics:**

| Tier | Metrics | How to read them |
|------|---------|------------------|
| Leading (actionable) | Load, reps, [RIR](rpe-rir-in-practice.md), weekly sets | Are my lifts progressing at target effort? Adjust the program from this. |
| Lagging (outcome) | Bodyweight trend, tape measurements, photos | Am I actually growing? **Direction** is readable at ~4 weeks; a verdict on the block needs **8–12+** — see [assessing progress](assessing-progress.md). |

**Steer with performance.** If your load and reps on key lifts are climbing over weeks at a controlled effort (RIR), you're almost certainly building muscle — this is the fastest, most reliable feedback. **Volume** (weekly hard sets) tells you whether you're doing enough work; **RIR** tells you whether it's hard enough.

**Making load × reps comparable.** Two sessions rarely differ in one variable, so you need a single number to rank them. The usual tool is an **estimated 1RM** — the Epley formula, `weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)`: 60 kg × 8 estimates 76 kg, 57.5 kg × 10 estimates 76.7 kg, so the second session was very slightly better. Three practical bounds, which are the ones this Bible's own engine applies:

| Reps in the set | How much to trust the estimate |
|---|---|
| 1–6 | **High** — closest to a real 1RM, the number to steer by |
| 7–10 | **Moderate** — usable for tracking, not for prescribing a true max |
| 11–12 | **Low** — a large extrapolation above the load you actually lifted |
| 13+ | **Don't** — a deliberately light 20-rep back-off set will "beat" a genuinely heavier triple. Track those by **top load at a given rep target** instead |

And treat a change smaller than about **0.5 kg** as noise rather than a record — an estimator wobble dressed up as a PR is how you talk yourself into a progression you didn't earn.

**Confirm with slow outcome measures.** Bodyweight (as a multi-week *trend*, not a single reading), a few consistent **tape measurements**, and **standardized photos** (same lighting, pose, time of day, unpumped) confirm the physique is changing. These are noisy in the short term, so only trust the direction over months. [Assessing progress](assessing-progress.md) covers how much of that noise is normal and what counts as a real change.

## The Evidence

**Progressive overload is the target, so performance is the key metric.** Growth depends on progressively increasing the training stimulus, and volume (hard sets) is its most practical dose measure[^schoenfeld-2017-volume-dose-response] — which is why logging load, reps, and set counts is the highest-value tracking. **[Grade A]** Baz-Valle and colleagues examined how training volume is best quantified and concluded that **counting hard sets per muscle per week** is the most practical valid method[^baz-valle-2021-counting-sets] — the reason this page recommends that unit rather than tonnage. **[Grade B]**

**Your RIR estimates are not equally good everywhere.** Zourdos and colleagues found that proximity to failure and the number of reps performed both influence how accurately lifters judge reps in reserve — estimates are markedly better close to failure and in lower-rep sets[^zourdos-2021-rir-accuracy]. **[Grade B]** So the effort number you log is most trustworthy exactly where the training matters most, and a "4 RIR" logged on a long set deserves less weight than a "1 RIR" on a heavy one. **Size measures are inherently noisy.** Haun and colleagues (2019) detailed how hypertrophy measurements differ by method and are confounded by short-term swelling and fluid shifts[^haun-2019-measurement] — the evidence basis for judging bodyweight/measurements over long windows and steering primarily by performance. **[Grade B]**

## Key Uncertainties & Nuance
- **Strength ≠ size perfectly** — early strength gains are neural, and load can rise faster than muscle; but sustained performance progress is still the best gym-available proxy for growth.
- **Measurement conditions matter** — inconsistent time-of-day, hydration, or "pump" ruin the signal in tape and photos.
- **Bodyweight combines muscle, fat, water, and gut content** — use it as a trend alongside measurements and photos, not alone.
- **What's easy to track (weight) isn't the most informative (performance)** — prioritize the actionable metric.

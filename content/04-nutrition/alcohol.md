# Alcohol

> **TL;DR** — Alcohol works against muscle growth, and the cost scales with the **dose**. About **1.5 g per kg of bodyweight** — roughly **12 standard drinks** for an 80 kg lifter — cut post-exercise muscle protein synthesis by **24%** even when protein was eaten alongside it. Around **0.5 g/kg** (about 3–4 drinks at that bodyweight) is unlikely to meaningfully affect recovery. You don't have to be a teetotaler to build muscle; you do have to keep the big nights away from your hard training.

**Quick recommendations**
- Keep post-training drinking at or under **~0.5 g/kg bodyweight** (≈3–4 standard drinks for an 80 kg lifter) — around that dose most aspects of recovery are unlikely to be affected. **[Grade C]**
- Treat **~1.5 g/kg** (≈12 drinks) as a genuine training cost: that dose cut post-exercise muscle protein synthesis by **24%** with protein and **37%** with carbohydrate. **[Grade B]**
- **Protein alongside the drinks does not rescue it** — eat protein anyway, but don't treat it as an antidote. **[Grade B]**
- Count the calories: alcohol carries **7 kcal per gram**, so a big night is easily several hundred kcal against your [energy balance](energy-balance.md), and it disrupts [sleep](../05-recovery/sleep.md). **[Grade C]**

## Practical Application

The useful question isn't "does alcohol hurt?" but "**how much, how close to training?**"

| Dose (80 kg lifter) | Grams per kg | What the evidence shows | Practical read |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~3–4 standard drinks | ~0.5 g/kg | Unlikely to impact most aspects of recovery[^barnes-2014-alcohol-recovery] | The realistic social ceiling on a training week |
| ~12 standard drinks | ~1.5 g/kg | Post-exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis **24% lower** with protein, **37% lower** with carbohydrate[^parr-2014-alcohol] | A session's adaptation substantially blunted |

Two practical rules follow. **Put the drinking as far from the hard session as you can** — the measured effect is on the hours *after* training, so a heavy night after leg day costs more than the same night on a rest day. And **don't count on food to fix it**: in the trial above, protein eaten with the alcohol still left protein synthesis 24% below the protein-only condition.

The indirect costs are often the bigger ones in practice. Alcohol degrades [sleep](../05-recovery/sleep.md) quality, and sleep is one of the few recovery inputs that genuinely moves the needle. It adds calories that count toward [energy balance](energy-balance.md) exactly like any other — at 7 kcal/g, ethanol sits between carbohydrate and fat, and it arrives alongside mixers and late-night food. And a poor night's sleep plus a hangover is one of the most common reasons a planned session gets skipped or dialled down, which over months costs more than any single blunted MPS response.

None of this makes alcohol a special poison. It makes it a **cost you should spend deliberately** rather than by accident.

## The Evidence

**Alcohol blunts the muscle-building response to training, and the dose is known.** Parr and colleagues (2014) had 8 physically active men complete resistance exercise followed by cycling, then consume either whey protein alone, alcohol (**1.5 g per kg bodyweight — reported as 12 ± 2 standard drinks**) with protein, or the same alcohol dose with carbohydrate. Compared with protein alone, myofibrillar protein synthesis was **24% lower** when alcohol was co-ingested with protein and **37% lower** with carbohydrate[^parr-2014-alcohol]. **[Grade B]** — a controlled crossover trial, but a small one, in a single session.

**Below roughly 0.5 g/kg the recovery cost appears small.** Barnes' review of alcohol in male athletes concludes that the impact on recovery depends on timing, injury status and dose, and that **a dose of approximately 0.5 g/kg bodyweight is unlikely to impact most aspects of recovery**[^barnes-2014-alcohol-recovery]. **[Grade C]** — a narrative review's practical recommendation rather than a measured threshold, so treat it as a sensible ceiling, not a safe-harbour guarantee.

The muscle-building process alcohol interferes with is described in [mechanisms](../00-foundations/mechanisms.md); the protein intake it fails to be rescued by is covered in [protein](protein.md).

## Key Uncertainties & Nuance
- **The 24%/37% figures come from one small crossover trial** (n = 8, one session). The direction is well supported; the exact magnitude for a different dose, drink or person is not.
- **0.5 g/kg is a review's recommendation, not a measured threshold.** No trial has established a dose below which the effect is reliably zero, and "unlikely to impact most aspects of recovery" is not "no effect".
- **The time course is not well pinned down.** How long after a session drinking stops mattering, and how quickly the effect fades, has not been established in trained lifters.
- **Acute MPS suppression is not a measured hypertrophy loss.** Nobody has run the long trial where matched lifters drink or don't for months, so the size of the real-world cost is inferred, not observed.
- **The indirect route may dominate.** Lost [sleep](../05-recovery/sleep.md), surplus calories and skipped sessions plausibly matter more over a year than the direct signalling effect — but that comparison hasn't been measured either.
- **Individual tolerance varies**, and none of this is a health recommendation. No amount of alcohol *helps* muscle growth.

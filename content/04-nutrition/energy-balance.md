# Energy Balance

> **TL;DR** — To gain muscle *maximally*, eat at a **slight calorie surplus** (~5–15% above maintenance) so your body has the energy and raw materials to build tissue. A surplus isn't strictly *required* — beginners, returning lifters, and those with higher body fat can gain muscle at maintenance or even in a deficit ("recomposition") — but for most trained lifters, a modest surplus supports faster gains. Keep it **modest**: a big surplus adds fat, not extra muscle.

**Quick recommendations**
- To gain lean mass fastest, run a **slight surplus**. That a surplus supports (but isn't strictly required for) maximal gain is **[Grade B]**; the specific **~+5–15% / +150–350 kcal/day** magnitude is a practical consensus heuristic, not a proven optimum[^iraki-2019-offseason-nutrition]. **[Grade C]**
- Keep the surplus **small** — muscle gain has a ceiling; excess calories become fat[^garthe-2013-nutrition-body-comp]. **[Grade B]**
- **Recomposition** (gain muscle, lose fat at ~maintenance) is realistic for **beginners, returnees, and higher-body-fat individuals**[^slater-2019-energy-surplus]. **[Grade B]**
- Adjust by **outcome**: track weight and measurements over weeks and nudge intake to hit ~0.25–0.5% bodyweight gain/week when bulking[^iraki-2019-offseason-nutrition]. **[Grade C]**

## Practical Application

**Set calories by goal:**

| Goal | Calorie target | Expect |
|------|----------------|--------|
| Maximize muscle gain | +5–15% over maintenance | ~0.25–0.5%/wk bodyweight gain; some fat gain |
| Recomposition | ~Maintenance | Slow simultaneous muscle gain + fat loss (best for beginners/higher body fat) |
| Lose fat, keep muscle | −10–20% deficit | See [Building Muscle in a Deficit](building-muscle-in-a-deficit.md) |

**Why modest.** The rate at which you can build muscle is capped by physiology, not by calorie intake — past a small surplus, extra calories don't accelerate muscle gain, they just add fat you'll later diet off. "Lean bulking" (a small surplus) keeps the muscle-to-fat ratio of your gains favorable and shortens future cutting phases.

**Adjust by the scale and mirror**, not by a formula. Maintenance estimates are rough; the real target is a gaining rate you can verify over 2–4 weeks. Gaining too fast → trim calories; not gaining → add some.

## The Evidence

**A surplus helps but isn't mandatory.** Slater and colleagues (2019) reviewed whether an energy surplus is required to maximize resistance-training hypertrophy and concluded that while a **surplus supports maximal muscle gain**, meaningful hypertrophy can still occur at energy balance or even in a deficit — particularly in **untrained, returning, or higher-body-fat individuals**, who can use body fat stores and have more "room" to add muscle[^slater-2019-energy-surplus]. **[Grade B]** The practical reading: bulk in a *slight* surplus to maximize the rate of gain, but understand that recomposition is a real option for the right person.

**Bigger isn't better — a controlled surplus partitions more favorably.** Garthe and colleagues had elite athletes gain weight during a strength-and-power block either with **structured nutritional guidance** or eating ad libitum; both groups increased lean mass, but the guided group **added more lean mass with less body-fat gain**, showing that a modest, managed surplus channels more of the extra calories into muscle rather than fat[^garthe-2013-nutrition-body-comp]. **[Grade B]** This is why "dirty bulking" backfires: past a small surplus, the extra energy mostly becomes fat you later have to diet off.

**How big, and how fast.** Iraki and colleagues' off-season nutrition review for bodybuilders translates this into working numbers — a **modest surplus (roughly +10–20% for leaner/less-experienced lifters, smaller for advanced/leaner ones) targeting about 0.25–0.5% bodyweight gain per week** — precisely so the muscle-to-fat ratio of the gain stays favorable[^iraki-2019-offseason-nutrition]. **[Grade C]** Treat these as starting heuristics to verify against the scale, not proven optima.

## Key Uncertainties & Nuance
- **"Maintenance calories" is an estimate**, and it changes as you gain weight and as activity shifts — recalculate from real-world results, not a calculator.
- **The optimal surplus size isn't precisely known**; +5–15% is a practical consensus range, not a proven optimum.
- **The "p-ratio"** (how much of a surplus becomes muscle vs fat) is influenced by training status, genetics, and body fat — leaner, trained individuals tend to partition surplus calories less favorably, arguing for a smaller surplus.
- **Body fat matters for recomp** — higher-body-fat individuals recompose more readily; very lean lifters usually need a surplus to gain.
